Project 2 · Corrective Colour Project

Colour Theory in Hair Design

Neutralizing brassy, over-processed hair with complementary colour theory.

Project Overview

This project focuses on corrective colour: using colour theory to fix brassy, over-processed hair by neutralizing unwanted warm tones with their complementary colours.

Why corrective colour: it shows colour theory most directly. Brass is unwanted warm pigment, and the fix is its exact opposite on the colour wheel. Orange is cancelled by blue, yellow by violet. That one rule connects every part of this project.

Part 1: Research and Theory

Introduction to Colour Theory

Colour theory is the set of rules that explains how colours mix and cancel each other. In the salon, it is what lets a colourist predict a result instead of hoping for one. Every toner, gloss and correction is colour theory in a bowl.

Primary colours are red, yellow and blue. They cannot be made by mixing other colours, and every hair pigment is built from them.

  • Blue is the strongest and darkest primary, but it is also the first to wash out. That's why a cool ash blonde slowly turns warmer between appointments, and why clients go home with a blue or violet shampoo.
  • Red is medium strength. It adds warmth and richness, and it's the pigment that makes brunettes look "too red" after lightening.
  • Yellow is the weakest primary and the hardest to remove. It's the last pigment left when hair is lightened, so even very light blondes need toning.

Secondary colours mix two primaries: red + yellow = orange, yellow + blue = green, blue + red = violet. Violet is in purple shampoo, green-based (matt) shades calm redness, and orange is the copper family.

Tertiary colours mix a primary with the secondary beside it: gold, copper, mahogany and blue-violet. These are the tones printed on colour tubes, so reading a shade chart is really reading the colour wheel.

Bold = primary · Medium = secondary · Light = tertiary · Opposites are complementary

The Colour Wheel and Colour Schemes

A colourist uses the wheel two ways: to cancel a tone the client doesn't want, and to build a look whose tones work together.

Warm and Cool Tones

Warm tones carry more red, orange or yellow: gold, copper, honey, auburn. Cool tones carry more blue, green or violet: ash, pearl, platinum, smoky beige. Neutral tones sit in between and suit the widest range of clients.

Warmth isn't a mistake, it's a choice. A client asking for "no warmth at all" needs to know that brunettes always reveal warmth when lightened, and that cool tones fade first. Setting that expectation at the consultation is part of applying colour theory.

Hair Colour Levels

Level is how light or dark the hair is, from 1 (black) to 10 (lightest blonde). When hair is lightened, the dark pigment breaks down first and leaves warm underlying pigment behind. Lightening a level 5 brunette to a level 9 passes through red, then orange, then yellow. Rinse too early and the client leaves orange, which is exactly what happens with box bleach at home.

Undertones in Hair Colour

Undertone is the tone sitting underneath the main colour. Two level 7 blondes can look completely different in window light: one glows golden, the other looks smoky. Warm undertones flash gold, copper or red; cool undertones look ashy or smoky; neutral sits in between.

Colour lines write tone after the level. In Wella, /0 is natural, /1 ash, /3 gold, /4 red, /6 violet and /8 pearl. So 8/81 is a level 8 with a pearl main tone and an ash secondary tone, and reading the number tells you which brass it will cancel before the tube is opened.

Skin Tones and Hair Types

Skin tone. Warm skin (golden, peach, olive) usually glows in honey, caramel and copper. Cool skin (pink, red or blue undertones) usually looks fresher in ash, beige, platinum and cool brown. Quick checks: veins look blue-purple on cool skin and green on warm skin, and clients usually already wear the metal that suits them. Always check in natural light.

Eye colour. Cool blondes brighten blue and grey eyes. Copper and gold bring out green and hazel eyes.

  • Fine hair processes faster and lifts easily, so check it often and consider a lower-volume developer.
  • Coarse or resistant hair takes longer to lift and may need more time.
  • Porous hair, like box-bleached ends, grabs colour fast. Ash can turn grey or green, so ends often get a softer formula or less time.
  • Healthy, low-porosity hair resists colour and can look patchy if rushed.

Psychology and Culture

A colour that works lifts a client's confidence the moment they see the mirror. One that clashes gets described as "I look tired" even when they can't say why. Warm tones read as approachable and energetic; cool tones read as polished and calm. Brass on cool skin looks flushed, and heavy ash on warm skin looks grey.

Most requests now arrive as an Instagram, TikTok or Pinterest photo, often filtered, so part of the consultation is explaining what's realistic. Celebrities drive trends fast, trends cycle (platinum, then "expensive brunette", then back), and seasons and workplaces shape choices too. A good colourist uses trends as inspiration and adjusts them to the client.

Colour Theory Principles Applied

Maya's brass was exposed underlying pigment: orange at level 7 and yellow-gold at level 8. Blue sits opposite orange, so I used a blue-based pearl-ash on the mids. Violet sits opposite yellow, so I mixed a custom pearl-ash and ash-violet blend for the ends. I chose a cool scheme because her fair, pink-toned skin and blue-grey eyes are cool, and the level 6 shadow root blended her regrowth softly.

Did the Final Colour Match the Client Profile?

Yes. The result was a soft, natural level 8 cool beige blonde with no visible orange or gold, and the shadow root melted into her regrowth. It brightens her blue-grey eyes, calms the redness in her skin, and lets her stretch appointments to the 8 to 10 weeks she wanted.

Adjustment made: at my 5-minute check, the porous ends were taking the violet blend much faster than the mids. I rinsed the ends at about 10 minutes instead of 15 while the mids kept processing. Without that change, the ends would have come out darker and greyer.

Challenge: the porous ends grabbed the toner

Porous hair has an open cuticle, so it absorbs colour faster and holds more of it, especially ash and violet. Checking every 5 minutes let me catch it early. Next time I'd treat porosity before toning and apply the toner to the mids first and the ends last.

How Colour Theory Influenced My Decisions

I read the brass before choosing anything. I identified orange in the mids and yellow on the ends, then matched each to its opposite, which is why I used two toners instead of one. It also taught me to treat the ends as their own zone, because the hair's condition decides how strongly it takes colour. Colour theory makes results predictable, and that's what I'll bring to real clients.

Personal statement

Technique first, then creativity

My name is Enzo Rizzuto, and I'm a hairstylist from Calgary, Alberta. I grew up around hair. My family runs Curios Hair, and being in the salon is how I first saw what a good stylist can do for someone's confidence. Watching a client leave feeling more like themselves is what made me want to take this work seriously.

This portfolio documents my term at NIWE Academy, where I'm completing my hairstyling training alongside my apprenticeship. Over ten weeks I worked through roller sets, perm wrapping, colour, up-dos and modern styling. Some of it came naturally. Some of it, like keeping clean sections and even tension on a roller set, took a lot of repetition before it looked the way I wanted.

My philosophy is simple. A style only looks effortless when the foundation underneath it is right: clean sections, the right tension and the right product. Once that's solid, I like to take classic techniques and finish them in a softer, more modern way that a client can actually wear.

My goal is to keep building my skills, especially in colour, and to grow my own clientele behind the chair. This portfolio is the starting point for that: a record of where my technique is today and how far it has come this term.
